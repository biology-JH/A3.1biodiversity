### Slide 10
- Variation as a Feature of Life
- Universal biological diversity across all clades
- Intraspecific Heterogeneity
- No two biological organisms are completely identical across all physical and biochemical traits. Even clones and monozygotic twins accumulate epigenetic differences and somatic mutations over their lifespans.
- Variation provides the raw substrate upon which natural selection acts. Systematic biology observes these patterns to construct valid taxonomic categories.
- A3.1.1 · SL

### Slide 11
- Continuous vs Discontinuous Variation
- Continuous Variation
- Controlled polygenically with significant environmental modification, displaying a spectrum with no distinct categories (e.g. human height).
- Discontinuous Variation
- Controlled by alleles of a single gene locus, unaffected by environment, exhibiting discrete qualitative classes (e.g. ABO blood groups).
- A3.1.1 · SL

### Slide 12
- Genome Unity and Diversity · A3.1.8–9 SL
- Within a species
- A genome is all the genetic information of an organism. Individuals of one species share most of their genome, yet differ at many positions.
- Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are variations at one DNA base. They contribute to genetic diversity within a species.
- Between species
- Eukaryote genomes differ in total DNA amount and base sequence. Differences between species are generally greater than differences within a species.

### Slide 14
- Morphological Species Concept · A3.1.2 SL
- Historically formalised by Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century, the morphological species concept defines a species as a group of organisms sharing structural and anatomical diagnostic characteristics.
- Before molecular genetics and gene sequencing, physical comparisons formed the sole empirical foundation for global biological taxonomy.

### Slide 15
- Strengths of Morphology
- KEY CONCEPT
- Morphological analysis remains the practical backbone of field ecology and museum taxonomy:
- Applies directly to fossilised palaeontological specimens devoid of intact ancient DNA.
- Enables rapid non-destructive field identification via dichotomous taxonomic keys.
- Accommodates obligate asexually reproducing organisms where breeding tests are impossible.
- A3.1.2 · SL | A3.1.12 · HL

### Slide 16
- Sexual Dimorphism
- Males and females of one species (e.g. peacock and peahen) appear morphologically distinct, risking false split classification.
- Cryptic Species
- Genetically isolated populations share identical external morphologies, misleading purely visual surveys.
- Phenotypic Plasticity
- Abiotic variables like altitude, nutrition, or sunlight drastically modify anatomy within identical genotypes.
- Convergent Evolution
- Unrelated evolutionary clades develop analogous physical forms due to parallel selection pressures.
- A3.1.2 · SL

### Slide 17
- Binomial System of Nomenclature · A3.1.3 SL
- Standardised Two-Part Naming
- The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN) and botanical equivalents mandate that every discovered organism receives a unique Latin binomial.
- Genus: First word; capitalised; identifies closely related species sharing distinct derived traits.
- Specific epithet: Second word; strictly lower case; identifies the exact individual species.

### Slide 18
- Universal Formatting Conventions · A3.1.3 SL
- 1

- 2
- Capitalisation Rules: The initial letter of the genus is capitalised; the specific epithet remains entirely in lowercase.
- 3
- Abbreviation Usage: After full citation in a text, the genus can be abbreviated to its initial capital letter (e.g. H. sapiens).

### Slide 22
- The Biological Species Concept
- KEY CONCEPT
- Ernst Mayr's Criterion (1942)
- A species is defined as a group of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups, producing viable, fertile offspring.
- Gene flow links populations of the same species into a cohesive, shared gene pool.
- A3.1.4 · SL

### Slide 23
- Challenges · A3.1.4 SL / A3.1.12 HL
- Asexual Organisms
- The biological species concept fits poorly: bacteria reproduce asexually, and horizontal gene transfer can move genes across species.
- Fossil Taxa
- Extinct organisms cannot be tested for reproductive compatibility, demanding anatomical proxy definitions.
- Fertile Hybrids
- Many distinct plant and bird species regularly hybridise in nature, producing fully fertile offspring.
- Allopatric Populations
- Geographically disjunct populations never meet in nature; assessing potential natural interbreeding is ambiguous.

### Slide 24
- Divergence During Speciation · A3.1.5 SL
- 1. Gene Flow
- A continuous population freely exchanges alleles across its geographic distribution.
- 2. Isolation
- Geographic or ecological separation prevents gene flow between isolated groups.
- 3. Speciation
- Mutation and selection cause divergence until post-zygotic barriers emerge.

### Slide 25
- The Speciation Continuum
- Gradual Divergence
- Speciation is rarely an instantaneous single event; it is a gradual continuum. As populations diverge, they accumulate behavioural, ecological, and physiological differences.
- 📌
- Definition · A3.1.5 SL
- Deciding whether two diverging populations are varieties or distinct species can be arbitrary.

### Slide 28
- Chromosome numbers · A3.1.6 SL / A3.1.13 HL
- Species-Specific Karyotypes
- Eukaryotes exhibit immense diversity in diploid chromosome number (2n). Because sexually reproducing species receive homologous pairs from two parents, diploid counts are normally even.
- Humans (Homo sapiens): 2n = 46 (23 pairs)
- Chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes): 2n = 48 (24 pairs)
- If closely related species have different chromosome numbers, their hybrids are unlikely to be fertile.

### Slide 29
- Environmental DNA and Barcodes · A3.1.15 HL
- Detecting hidden biodiversity
- Organisms leave DNA in their habitat through cells, mucus, faeces and other material. Researchers can collect environmental DNA (eDNA) from samples such as water or soil.
- DNA barcodes are short sequences compared with reference databases to identify species. This can survey a habitat’s biodiversity rapidly, including species that are difficult to see or catch.
- Reliable identification depends on good samples and accurate reference sequences.

### Slide 30
- Karyotyping and Karyograms · A3.1.7 SL
- KEY CONCEPT
- Standard Classification Criteria
- In a completed karyogram, arrested metaphase chromosomes are cut out and paired into homologues by cytogeneticists based on three primary anatomical features:
- Length: Ordered strictly from longest to shortest pairs.
- Centromere position: Metacentric, submetacentric, or acrocentric.
- Banding pattern: Reproducible dark/light G-banding.

### Slide 31
- Centromere Position and Morphology
- Anatomical Landmarks
- The centromere divides the chromosome into a short arm (p arm, from French petit) and a long arm (q arm). Centromeric index precisely identifies chromosomes under light microscopy.
- Metacentric: Centromere situated centrally (p \approx q).
- Submetacentric: Offset from centre (p < q).
- Acrocentric: Located near terminus with satellite DNA.
- A3.1.7 · SL

### Slide 32
- The Origin of Human Chromosome 2 · A3.1.7 SL
- The Chromosomal Disparity Paradox
- All great apes—chimpanzees, gorillas, and orangutans—possess 48 chromosomes (2n = 48), whereas humans have 46 (2n = 46).
- Did the human lineage lose an entire ancestral chromosome pair, or did two ancestral acrocentric chromosomes undergo an end-to-end telomeric fusion?

### Slide 33
- Evidence for Chromosome 2 Fusion · A3.1.7 SL
- 1
- G-Banding Homology: Human chromosome 2 banding patterns match chimps 2A and 2B laid end-to-end.
- 2
- Internal Telomeric Repeats: Inverted \text{(TTAGGG)}_n telomere repeats exist mid-chromosome (band 2q13).
- 3
- Vestigial Centromere: A second, inactive centromere remnant with alphoid satellite DNA rests on 2q21.

### Slide 34
- NOS: Testable Hypotheses · A3.1.7 SL
- Testable Hypothesis
- Proposes an empirical mechanism yielding predictions falsifiable by observation (e.g. chromosome 2 fusion predicting head-to-head telomeres).
- Non-Testable Assertion
- Involves unfalsifiable metaphysical assertions or subjective beliefs beyond empirical refutation or physical verification.

### Slide 6

- Variation is the basis of classification
- No two organisms are identical in every trait. Variation within populations is a defining feature of life and underpins classification.

### Slide 7
- Binomial Nomenclature A3.1.3 · SL & HL
- A shared two-part naming system
- Each scientific name has a genus name followed by a species epithet. The genus begins with a capital letter; the epithet is lower-case. Both are italicised when printed: Panthera leo.
- Species in the same genus share traits. The second part distinguishes one species from others in that genus.

### Slide 8

### Slide 9

- Counts vary among species
- Plant and animal species differ in chromosome number. Humans have 46 chromosomes in diploid body cells; chimpanzees have 48.
- Diploid cells have two chromosome sets, so their chromosome total is even. A count alone does not establish species identity.

### Slide 13

- KEY CONCEPT

### Slide 14
- SNPs: Single-Base Variation A3.1.8 · SL
- A SNP is a single-base difference found in at least 1% of a population.
- In humans, SNPs occur about once every 1,000 base pairs.
- Most occur in non-coding DNA and have no effect on phenotype.
- Some exonic SNPs change codons and protein structure or function.

### Slide 15
- Diversity of Eukaryote Genomes A3.1.9 · SL
- Genome Size Range
- Total nuclear DNA content varies by over 60,000-fold across eukaryotes, from 12 Mbp in unicellular yeast to over 150,000 Mbp in rare ferns.
- Base Sequence Divergence
- Nucleotide substitution rates accumulate proportionally to evolutionary divergence time, yielding far greater differences between species than within.

### Slide 17
- The C-Value Paradox A3.1.10 · SL
- KEY CONCEPT
- No Direct Correlation Between DNA Mass and Organisation
- Genome size does not correspond to an organism's morphological complexity or number of functional genes. The human genome contains approximately 3,200 megabase pairs (Mbp) and ~20,000 protein-coding genes, yet the tiny protozoan Amoeba proteus carries an estimated 290,000 Mbp.
- 📌
- Definition
- The C-value paradox describes the lack of correlation between DNA content and structural complexity.

### Slide 18
- Why Do Genomes Vary in Size? A3.1.9 · SL
- Transposons
- Mobile DNA copies and inserts across chromosomes, adding non-coding DNA.
- Tandem Repeats
- Tandem repeats form large non-coding DNA regions.
- Polyploidy
- Whole-genome duplication adds chromosome sets, especially in plants.
- Intron Expansion
- Long introns between exons increase genome size.

### Slide 26

- Transformation: Direct uptake of naked exogenous environmental DNA.

- HGT converts the tree of life into an interconnected web, obscuring clear phylogenetic boundaries.

- A Shared Species Trait

### Slide 31
- Interspecific Hybrids: The Mule A3.1.13 HL
- The horse (Equus caballus) carries 2n = 64 chromosomes (n = 32 gamete). The donkey (Equus asinus) carries 2n = 62 (n = 31 gamete).
- A cross produces a mule with 2n = 63 chromosomes.
- During gametogenesis, the unpaired 63rd chromosome and lack of homologue homology prevents stable bivalent synapsis.
- Meiotic arrest results in complete hybrid sterility.

### Slide 32
